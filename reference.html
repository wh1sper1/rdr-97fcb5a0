Words worth knowing

Politics runs on words until they mean nothing. This page fixes the meanings: what each term describes, where it came from, and what it does not mean. Every entry carries its own source, so you can check the definition instead of taking it on trust. The history sections below do the same job with chronology: the documented record of Israel and Palestine, in order.

A definition is not an argument. These entries tell you what a word means so the argument can be had honestly, and they do not settle whether a movement or a government is good or bad. Where a claim is genuinely contested, the entry says so.

The sources here are reference works, governments and human rights organizations, not opinion columns. Spotted an error, or want a term added? Use the contact link at the bottom of any page.

Fascism and the far right
Ideology

Fascism

An ultranationalist politics that rejects democracy, liberalism and equality, concentrates power in one leader or one party, and sells the nation as a people reborn through purification and force. Mussolini's Italy is the original. Hitler's Germany is the most lethal example.

What historians look for: a story of national decline followed by rebirth, a leader who stands above the law, uniformed fighters in the street, a rotating list of internal enemies blamed for the decline (Jews, migrants, communists, unions, the press, queer people), and open contempt for elections and courts as obstacles. Not every authoritarian is a fascist, and not every policy anyone dislikes is fascism. The word names this specific package, and using it for everything is how it stops meaning anything.

Source: Encyclopedia Britannica, Fascism

Variant

Nazism

The ideology and the one-party rule of the Nazi Party in Germany from 1933 to 1945, built on racial hierarchy: a supposed Aryan master race at the top, Jews cast as a racial enemy to be removed. Antisemitism was not a side policy, it was the core. The Nuremberg Laws of 1935 stripped Jews of citizenship, then came ghettos and confiscation, then the murder of six million Jews in the Holocaust, alongside Roma, disabled people, Slavic civilians, political prisoners and gay men.

Nazism is a specific case of fascism, not a synonym for it and not unrelated to it either. Fascism is the wider family: ultranationalism, one leader, contempt for democracy. Nazism is that family's racial theory taken to genocide, plus a war of conquest in Eastern Europe built on the idea of living space.

Source: Encyclopedia Britannica, Nazism

Current

The far right and the alt-right

The far right is the part of the spectrum that rejects liberal democracy, equal citizenship and pluralism, and wants society arranged by hierarchy: race, religion, nation or tradition. It includes fascist and neo-Nazi groups, white nationalists, armed paramilitaries and religious extremists, and also the radical right that stands in elections while still refusing those basics.

The alt-right is the American packaging of white nationalism for the internet age, popularized between roughly 2010 and 2016: memes, irony, identitarian language, and the claim to be beyond conservatism and beyond liberalism. It rebrands what it descends from. A right-wing voter is not automatically far right. The line is where democracy and equal citizenship get rejected.

Source: Encyclopedia Britannica, Alt-right

Race

White nationalism and white supremacy

White supremacy is the claim that white people are superior and should dominate society. Its record is slavery and Jim Crow in the United States, colonial rule across Africa and Asia, apartheid South Africa, and the Nazi state.

White nationalism is the politics that follows from it: white people are a nation and need their own state or territory, separated from everyone else. Ethnostate proposals, Klan separatism and the accelerationist groups that want a race war all sit in this family. Both terms rest on one core claim: race decides a person's worth and a person's belonging.

Source: Encyclopedia Britannica, White supremacy

Prejudice

Antisemitism

Hostility or prejudice against Jews as Jews, whether they are treated as a religion, an ethnicity, or both. Its history runs from medieval expulsions and blood libels through nineteenth century racial science and the Dreyfus affair to the Holocaust. Today it shows up as Holocaust denial, attacks on synagogues, conspiracy theories about Jewish control of finance and the media, and abuse aimed at Jews for what Israel's government does.

The IHRA working definition, adopted by the United States, the European Union and dozens of other countries, describes it as a certain perception of Jews that may be expressed as hatred, with worked examples. It also states that criticism of Israel like that leveled at any other country is not antisemitic, while blaming Jews as a group for Israel's policy is. That line matters, because both sides of the argument over Israel reach for the charge.

Source: United States State Department, Defining Antisemitism

The left and its opposition
Opposition

Anti-fascism (Antifa)

Not an organization. There is no membership, no leadership, no dues and no spokesperson. Antifa is a loose tradition of direct action against far-right organizing: the position that fascism should be stopped in the street before it wins elections, rather than handed to the police and the courts to deal with once it already has.

It descends from the anti-fascist groups that fought the Nazis in Weimar Germany, including the Communist Party's Antifaschistische Aktion in 1932, and it revived in the 1980s against skinhead neo-Nazis and again in the United States after 2016. Tactics run from counter-protest and exposing violent organizers to, at the fringes, fighting and property destruction. Because it is a tendency rather than an institution, nobody joins it and nobody speaks for it.

Source: Encyclopedia Britannica, Antifa

The left

Socialism

The tradition that wealth produced by workers should be owned socially and run democratically: by the workers themselves, by co-operatives, by communities, or by a state answerable to the public, instead of by private owners who keep the profit. It grew in industrial Europe in the early nineteenth century, when the factory made the gap between who does the work and who gets the money impossible to miss.

It splits into currents that are not interchangeable. Revolutionary socialism wants the system replaced. Democratic socialism works through elections and mixes public and co-operative ownership with private enterprise. Social democracy, the Nordic model, keeps capitalism and then taxes and regulates it hard. American politics calls any public program socialism. That is not what the word means in political science.

Source: Encyclopedia Britannica, Socialism

The left

Communism

In theory, a classless and stateless society where the means of production are held in common and people contribute and take according to need. Marx and Engels put it into modern politics in The Communist Manifesto in 1848, as the end point of history's class struggles, reached by revolution in most of its strands.

In practice, every state that has called itself communist has been a one-party state ruled by a party claiming to act for the working class, usually with a command economy, no political pluralism and, in the worst cases, mass terror. Those governments claimed to be on the way to communism. Whether they ever got close is the argument historians actually have, and judging the theory only by them assumes the answer in advance.

Source: Encyclopedia Britannica, Communism

Who actually rules
Power

Authoritarianism

A political system where power sits in one leader or a small group with almost no checks: elections that cannot remove them, courts that will not rule against them, press that cannot criticize them safely, opposition that gets punished. Most citizens keep their private lives, which is what separates it from totalitarianism, where the state also wants to control what you believe.

Most dictatorships today are authoritarian rather than totalitarian. They want obedience more than conviction, and they leave enough private life for the economy to keep running. The test that matters is simple: can the people at the top be removed without violence?

Source: Encyclopedia Britannica, Authoritarianism

Power

Totalitarianism

The stronger form of dictatorship: a state that does not only demand obedience but goes after belief itself, through one official ideology, one party, a secret police, a controlled press, and control of schools, unions, churches and family life. Nazi Germany and the Soviet Union under Stalin are the textbook cases, and the word was coined to describe them.

Totalitarianism and authoritarianism are not interchangeable. Authoritarian rulers want you quiet. Totalitarian rulers want you convinced. Almost nothing today is fully totalitarian, and the distinction matters because the two systems fail and get dismantled in different ways.

Source: Encyclopedia Britannica, Totalitarianism

Power

Oligarchy

Rule by a small group, whether they got there through wealth, family, military rank or party position. The term goes back to Aristotle. Robert Michels argued in 1911 that every organization, even democratic ones, ends up run by a leadership caste that starts wanting to keep its own position more than it wants the group's goals.

Modern cases are well documented: Russia after 2000, where a handful of industrialists, security officials and state firms orbit one leader; post-Soviet states built on captured industries; oil states where one family owns the resource. The test is whether that group can be voted out or prosecuted. If it cannot, it is an oligarchy.

Source: Encyclopedia Britannica, Oligarchy

Power

Plutocracy

Rule by the wealthy. Stronger than the everyday observation that money buys influence: policy follows what the rich want regardless of what everybody else wants. The classic modern study of this, by Martin Gilens and Benjamin Page in 2014, tested 1,779 policy questions in the United States and found that economic elites and organized business groups had substantial independent influence over what the government did, while average citizens had little or none.

A country can be a democracy on paper and a plutocracy in practice. Those two descriptions are not opposites, and that is the whole point of having the word.

Source: Encyclopedia Britannica Dictionary, Plutocracy · Gilens and Page, Perspectives on Politics (2014)

Power

Kleptocracy

Rule by theft. The people in office treat the state as a looting machine: ministries and contracts sold, public budgets routed to family and friends, public money moved into personal and offshore accounts. Unlike ordinary corruption, the stealing is the reason to hold the power.

Documented examples include Nigeria under Sani Abacha, the Philippines under Ferdinand Marcos, and the offshore wealth revealed for Russian officials in the Panama and Pandora Papers leaks. Kleptocracy also travels. Stolen money needs banks, lawyers and property markets that do not ask where it came from.

Source: Encyclopedia Britannica, Kleptocracy

Power

Corporatocracy

A society run for the benefit of large corporations, where government policy is written with, and often by, the industries it is supposed to oversee: lobbying that writes the draft, the revolving door between regulators and the firms they regulate, campaign cash on both sides, and rules only a giant could afford to meet.

Do not confuse it with corporatism, the older political science term for organizing society into official bodies: employers' associations, unions and the state negotiating together, which Fascist Italy claimed as its model. One is rule by corporations, a concentration of power with no electorate behind it. The other is a structure of bargaining. They share a root and nothing else.

Source: Merriam-Webster, corporatocracy · Encyclopedia Britannica, Corporatism

Power

Regulatory capture

When the industry an agency is supposed to oversee ends up running the agency: the same firms help write the rules, staff the office through the revolving door, fund the studies, and hire the lawyers who argue the cases. The idea is old in economics. George Stigler argued in 1971 that regulation is usually sought by the regulated, because a concentrated industry can afford to lobby and diffuse consumers cannot.

The tell is not bribery. It is a public agency whose decisions consistently track the preferences of the companies it watches, in language only those companies had the staff to draft. Clean campaign finance does not fix it, because the payment arrives as careers, access and information rather than cash.

Source: George Stigler, The Theory of Economic Regulation (1971) · Georgetown Law, Industry Capture of the Executive

Data

Surveillance capitalism

A business model that treats personal data as raw material: take more than the person offered, use it to predict what they will do next, then sell the prediction or use it to steer the behavior itself. The term was coined by the Harvard professor Shoshana Zuboff in 2019 for markets that trade in what she called human futures.

It is the logic behind a camera network that starts as a safety feature and ends as a permanent, searchable record of who went where: plate readers, facial recognition, the data exhaust of everyday apps. Collection for one purpose becomes sale, sharing and policing for another.

Source: Encyclopedia Britannica, Surveillance capitalism · Harvard Gazette interview with Shoshana Zuboff

Power

Coup and self-coup

A coup d'état is the sudden, illegal removal of a government by a small group, usually the military or the security services, without going through the constitution's own procedures. Elections, courts and legislatures are not consulted, because the point is that the usual routes get bypassed. Analysts have counted hundreds of attempts around the world since 1950, and coup governments rarely deliver what they promise.

A self-coup is when the people already in power do it to themselves: the president dissolves parliament, suspends the constitution and rules by decree, turning the state against its own rules. Fujimori in Peru in 1992 is the standard case, and self-coup attempts have kept appearing since. It is worth its own name because the uniforms stay home and it is still a coup.

Source: Encyclopedia Britannica, Coup d'etat · Council on Foreign Relations, Coups and Other Irregular Leadership Changes

How the story gets told
Persuasion

Demagogue

A politician who wins support by appealing to fear, resentment and prejudice instead of to argument, and who treats the crowd's mood as the only authority there is. The word is older than today's troubles: Aristotle wrote about the type, and Athenian voters knew what one looked like.

The pattern is consistent: simple answers to complicated problems, a list of outsiders who are to blame, praise for the crowd as the real people and contempt for anything that checks the leader (courts, press, civil service), and flattery that turns to fury the moment the crowd stops applauding. Detailed policy is not part of the act.

Source: Britannica Dictionary, demagogue

Persuasion

Populism

A style of politics that claims to speak for a pure, hard working people against a rotten elite. It comes in left and right versions, and in democracies and dictatorships, which is why political scientists treat it as a strategy rather than an ideology: the move that matters is splitting society into the good people and a corrupt establishment.

It is not a synonym for fascism and not an insult by definition. A populist can be democrat or authoritarian, and some populist movements check power instead of grabbing it. The question to ask is what the leader wants to do with the institutions in the way: work with them, or break them.

Source: Encyclopedia Britannica, Populism

Persuasion

Misinformation and disinformation

Misinformation is false or misleading content spread without intent to deceive: a wrong figure passed along in good faith. Disinformation is the same content spread on purpose, to profit, to discredit or to destabilize. The difference is the intent, not the words, and one rumor can be both depending on who is carrying it.

The machinery is cheap: fake accounts, recycled footage with a fresh caption, out of context quotes, and outlets that launder a claim until it looks reported. The defenses are boring and effective: find who benefits, locate the original, wait for a second source, and treat the claim that confirms everything you already believe with more suspicion rather than less.

Source: Encyclopedia Britannica, Misinformation and disinformation

Persuasion

Deep state

Two different things wearing one name. In political science it is a durable network inside the state (officers, intelligence services, senior civil servants) that steers policy from behind elected government, a real and documented pattern in countries from Pakistan to Turkey. In American argument it is usually an accusation: unnamed bureaucrats sabotaging a president.

Both uses point at the same real question, which is how much of government is decided by people nobody elected. Answering it needs evidence of specific coordination, not the phrase. On its own it can name genuine institutional resistance, or it can be used to discredit any official who refuses an order, and only the evidence tells you which one you are looking at.

Source: Merriam-Webster, deep state · Political Studies Association, What is a deep state?

Race, nation and land
Race law

Apartheid

Literally apartness: a legal system that sorts people by race and then rules through that sorting. South Africa built one from 1948 to 1991: the population classified by race, land and residence restricted by law, marriage criminalized across the color line, Black South Africans forced to carry passes, opposition crushed. It ended through internal resistance and international pressure, not through reform handed down from above.

The legal definition matters, because the word is defined in international law as an institutionalized system of oppression and domination by one racial group over another, set up to keep that regime in place. It is not a South Africa only word. In 2022 a United Nations Special Rapporteur concluded that Israel's rule over Palestinians meets that definition. B'Tselem, Human Rights Watch and Amnesty International had each reached the same conclusion earlier from their own investigations.

Source: Encyclopedia Britannica, Apartheid · United Nations, Special Rapporteur press release (2022)

Law

Genocide

A legal term with a definition written in 1948: acts committed with intent to destroy, in whole or in part, a national, ethnical, racial or religious group as such. The convention lists five acts: killing members of the group, causing serious bodily or mental harm, deliberately inflicting conditions of life meant to bring destruction, preventing births, and forcibly transferring children of the group to another one.

Two parts decide any case: one of those acts, plus the specific intent to destroy the group as such. That intent is why prosecutions take years and why the word is fought over in live conflicts, including matters now before international courts. States rarely announce it, so courts read intent from orders, scale, pattern and the statements of those carrying it out.

Source: Encyclopedia Britannica, Genocide · United Nations, 1948 Genocide Convention

National movement

Zionism

The national movement of the Jewish people, born in late nineteenth century Europe, when pogroms kept making the case that emancipation had failed and that a homeland of their own was the answer. Political Zionism organized for it, labor Zionism built kibbutzim and unions, revisionist Zionism put military strength first. The state of Israel was founded in 1948. The same year is the Nakba, in which roughly 700,000 Palestinians were displaced or fled.

It is not one thing. Israelis and diaspora Jews hold Zionist and anti-Zionist positions alike, and the label gets used as a self description and as an insult, often carelessly. As a definition it is support for Jewish self-determination in that land. Whether the project has been just in practice is the real argument, and it gets settled on the record: 1948, the 1967 occupation, the settlements, and how people live under military rule.

Source: Encyclopedia Britannica, Zionism

Rights and democracy
Rights

Free speech and the First Amendment

In the United States the First Amendment says Congress shall make no law abridging freedom of speech, and courts read it to cover speech, press, religion, assembly and petition. Its most important limit for everyday arguments: it restricts the government, not private platforms, employers or neighbors. A social media company removing a post is not a constitutional violation, while a city doing it might be.

It is not absolute either. The classic unprotected categories are true threats, incitement to imminent lawless action, fraud, defamation and obscenity, and time, place and manner rules can apply to anyone. Political speech gets the strongest protection, which is exactly the speech people in power most want restricted.

Source: Cornell Legal Information Institute, First Amendment · United States Courts, What Does Free Speech Mean?

Votes

Gerrymandering

Drawing electoral districts so one party's votes turn into more seats: pack the other side's voters into a few overwhelming districts, and crack the rest across many where they fall just short. The name comes from Elbridge Gerry, a Massachusetts governor whose 1812 district was said to look like a salamander.

Modern mapmaking works from block level voting data, which is why a party can lose the statewide vote and still keep the legislature. Racial gerrymandering is a separate legal question in US law, since race cannot be the predominant factor in drawing lines, though proving and policing that has proven hard.

Source: Encyclopedia Britannica, Gerrymandering

Israel and Palestine: the history

This is the record, not the framing. Where an event is documented it is stated as documented, where a court or a UN body ruled it is stated as a ruling with its date, and where numbers move the source that carries the current count is named instead of a frozen figure.

The Israeli account of 1948 as a war of independence and the Palestinian account of 1948 as an expulsion are not treated as equally supported. The flight and removal of about 700,000 people is documented by Israeli historians as well as Palestinian ones, and both names describe the same year. The sources below are Israeli human rights organizations, the International Court of Justice, the United Nations and reference works. Read them and disagree with any part of it on the evidence.

1880s to 1917

A place with people already on it

Palestine spent four centuries under Ottoman rule as an Arab land of farming villages, port towns and olive and citrus groves, with a mostly Muslim and Christian population and a small indigenous Jewish community. In the 1880s, waves of pogroms in the Russian empire turned political Zionism into an organized project: Jewish immigration began, land was bought through European-backed funds, and the peasant tenants on that land were frequently removed once it changed hands. By 1914 the immigrant community was a fact on the ground rather than a plan on paper.

In 1917 Britain promised a national home for the Jewish people in Palestine, while stating that nothing should prejudice the civil and religious rights of the existing non-Jewish communities. Both halves of that sentence were quoted by every side afterwards as a promise to itself. Britain took the mandate in 1920 and spent the next three decades managing the contradiction with immigration quotas, land rules and troops, until the mandate broke under the weight of the Holocaust and the revolt against it.

Source: Encyclopedia Britannica, Balfour Declaration

1936 to 1948

Partition, war, and the Nakba

The Arab revolt of 1936 to 1939 shook the mandate, and Britain answered by limiting Jewish immigration on the eve of the Holocaust, a policy the Zionist leadership treated as a betrayal. After the war, survivors in displaced persons camps and clandestine ship arrivals made Palestine ungovernable. In November 1947 the General Assembly voted to split the country into a Jewish and an Arab state, resolution 181, allotting the proposed Jewish state about 55 per cent of the land while Jews were roughly a third of the population. The Arab leadership and the surrounding states rejected the plan and fighting started immediately.

Israel declared independence in May 1948 and the neighboring armies entered. The war ended with Israel holding far more territory than partition had offered: about 78 per cent of mandatory Palestine, with Gaza under Egyptian military rule and the West Bank under Jordan. The Palestinian side calls that year the Nakba, the catastrophe: around 700,000 people expelled or fled, hundreds of villages emptied and many of them demolished, and no right of return granted. Their descendants now number in the millions. Resolution 194 of December 1948 says those who wish to live in peace with their neighbors should be allowed to return to their homes, and it has never been implemented.

Source: Encyclopedia Britannica, United Nations Resolution 181 · Encyclopedia Britannica, 1948 Arab-Israeli War · United Nations, Right of return of the Palestinian people

June 1967

The occupation starts

In June 1967 Israel fought Egypt, Jordan and Syria and in six days took the West Bank, Gaza, East Jerusalem, the Sinai Peninsula and the Golan Heights. The Security Council answered within months with resolution 242, which put two rules in writing: territory cannot be acquired by war, and a just peace must include withdrawal. The resolution was drafted with a deliberate ambiguity about how much withdrawal, and every peace framework since has argued over that gap.

The military government that went into the West Bank and Gaza was described as temporary. What it built is still running: permits, land confiscation, demolition orders and settlements. The first settlements went up within a year of the war, and the Fourth Geneva Convention, which an occupying power is bound by and which bars that power from moving its own population into the territory it occupies, has been the legal reference point ever since. Israel argues the convention does not apply to these territories. Every other body that has ruled on it has disagreed.

Source: Encyclopedia Britannica, Six-Day War · United Nations, Security Council Resolution 242 (1967)

1967 to 2025

Settlers, courts, and the word apartheid

Settlements grew from a security argument into a fact on the ground. The Secretary-General's report of September 2025 counts 737,332 settlers in the West Bank and East Jerusalem, across 141 settlements in the West Bank and 25 in East Jerusalem. The International Court of Justice has ruled twice: in 2004 that the separation wall and the settlements it serves are unlawful, and in July 2024 that Israel's continued presence in the territory is unlawful, that it must end that presence as rapidly as possible, stop all new settlement activity, evacuate the settlers and pay reparations.

The word apartheid arrived through investigation rather than rhetoric. B'Tselem, Israel's own human rights organization, reached that conclusion in January 2021; Human Rights Watch in April 2021; Amnesty International in February 2022; and a United Nations special rapporteur in 2022. Each of them applied the legal definition from the 1973 convention and the Rome Statute to the same record of laws and practices, and each described a single regime between the river and the sea with different rights for two peoples under it. That four independent bodies, including one inside Israel, reached the same word is the part worth arguing from.

Source: United Nations, Secretary-General report on settlements (September 2025) · International Court of Justice, Advisory Opinion press release (July 2024) · B'Tselem, This is apartheid (2021) · Human Rights Watch, A Threshold Crossed (2021) · Amnesty International, Israel's apartheid against Palestinians (2022) · United Nations, Special Rapporteur on Palestine (2022)

1993 to 2005

Oslo, and what replaced it

Oslo I in 1993 brought mutual recognition: Israel recognized the PLO, the PLO recognized Israel, and a Palestinian Authority was created to govern parts of the West Bank and Gaza while the hard questions were pushed to final status talks: Jerusalem, refugees, borders, settlements, security. The territory was sorted into areas A, B and C, and area C, roughly 60 per cent of the West Bank, stayed under full Israeli control. Rabin signed the agreement and was assassinated in November 1995 by an Israeli extremist opposed to it.

The final status talks collapsed at Camp David in 2000 and the second intifada followed, with suicide bombings inside Israel and a reoccupation of West Bank cities by the army. Hamas won the Palestinian legislative election in 2006, and the Fatah and Hamas split of 2007 left Gaza under Hamas and the West Bank under the Authority. Settlement construction ran through every one of those years. Oslo did not fail in a single moment, it was emptied out over a decade while the map changed underneath it.

Source: Encyclopedia Britannica, Oslo Accords · Encyclopedia Britannica, Hamas

2007 to 2023

Gaza, walled and bombed

Israel pulled its settlers and soldiers out of Gaza in 2005 and two years later, after Hamas won the election and took the strip, imposed a land and sea blockade, with Egypt controlling the southern crossing at Rafah. The result was described by the UN and every aid agency as a lock on the population: a few hours of electricity a day, water and sewage systems failing, and unemployment among the highest anywhere. Movement in and out shrank to permits for medical and humanitarian cases.

The blockade was enforced by repeated wars: December 2008, November 2012, July 2014, May 2021, each killing thousands of Palestinians and hundreds of Israelis and flattening sections of Gaza that were then rebuilt. In 2018 Palestinians marched to the fence in what they called the Great March of Return. A UN commission investigated every killing at the protest sites and found that 183 of 189 Palestinians killed were shot with live ammunition, that only in one case was there an imminent threat, and that the rest of the shootings were unlawful under international law.

Source: Encyclopedia Britannica, Gaza Strip: Blockade · United Nations, Commission of inquiry on the 2018 Gaza protests

7 October 2023 onward

What came after 7 October

On 7 October 2023 Hamas and other armed groups broke out of Gaza, killed about 1,200 people in southern Israel and took roughly 250 hostages. Attacking civilians and taking hostages are war crimes, and the killings and the sexual violence documented that day were condemned clearly and should be. So should the demand that the hostages be freed.

What followed was a war waged on a strip holding 2.3 million people. By the UN's own counts it is the deadliest phase of the conflict by a wide margin: tens of thousands of Palestinians killed, with women and children making up the majority according to UN agencies, the population displaced from north to south and back again, and whole neighborhoods along with hospitals, universities, water and sewage systems destroyed. Famine conditions were confirmed in parts of Gaza by the UN-backed famine review system, and aid access was restricted at the crossings Israel controls. Those counts move weekly, so the UN's OCHA page carries the current figure rather than this section.

The courts moved faster than the diplomacy. In January 2024 the International Court of Justice, hearing a genocide case brought by South Africa, ordered Israel to prevent genocide in Gaza and to allow humanitarian assistance, and ordered further measures for Rafah in May; that case continues. In November 2024 the International Criminal Court issued arrest warrants for Benjamin Netanyahu and Yoav Gallant for war crimes and crimes against humanity, including starvation as a method of warfare, and rejected Israel's challenge to its jurisdiction. In July 2024 the world court told the General Assembly that Israel's presence in the territory is unlawful and must end as rapidly as possible.

Source: Encyclopedia Britannica, October 7 attack · International Criminal Court, arrest warrants (November 2024) · International Court of Justice, Advisory Opinion press release (July 2024) · United Nations OCHA, occupied Palestinian territory

Putin and Russian aggression: the history

The source list below is deliberate. Russian state media and Kremlin statements are not used as evidence of what Russian forces did in Ukraine, because they are party to the question. What carries this section is the United Nations monitoring mission, the International Criminal Court, the UN General Assembly, the Russian human rights group OVD-Info, and reference works.

Where an attribution is still contested by the parties, it is left out rather than smoothed over. The front line moves every day, so this section stops at what is documented and sends the present tense to the briefs and the war map.

1999 to 2004

How the system was built

Vladimir Putin was appointed prime minister in August 1999. Three weeks later apartment buildings were blown up in Moscow and other cities, killing hundreds, and the war against Chechnya that followed became the vehicle for his rise. Grozny was reduced to rubble over the winter, the conflict was sold on television as a counterterrorism operation, and he won the presidency in March 2000 with the main channels already brought to heel.

The next four years set the pattern that everything after it followed: independent television taken over (NTV seized in 2001), the oligarchs exiled or jailed, most notably Khodorkovsky arrested in 2003, regional governors stripped of independence, and the press narrowed to a managed consensus. In 2008 he swapped jobs with Medvedev to keep the office in 2012. By the time he looked abroad, the domestic checks had already been removed, and war had already been proven to work as politics.

Source: Encyclopedia Britannica, Vladimir Putin · BBC, Chechnya timeline

2007 to 2014

Warnings, then Crimea

At the Munich security conference in 2007 Putin attacked the post Cold War order as a one man game run by Washington. In August 2008 Russian forces fought Georgia and stopped short of Tbilisi, leaving South Ossetia and Abkhazia under Russian control and formally recognized, a template for what came later: seize, recognize, hold. At home the 2011 and 2012 election protests were answered with new repression laws.

In February 2014 Ukraine's president fled after months of protest and Russian troops without insignia took Crimea within days. The March referendum was rejected by the UN General Assembly on 27 March 2014, 100 votes to 11 with 58 abstentions, which affirmed Ukraine's borders and called the vote invalid. Fighting then began in the Donbas with Russian weapons, money and fighters, and on 17 July 2014 Malaysia Airlines flight 17 was shot down over the region killing all 298 people aboard; investigators traced the launcher to a Russian brigade.

Source: Encyclopedia Britannica, South Ossetia · United Nations, General Assembly resolution on Crimea (2014) · Encyclopedia Britannica, Malaysia Airlines flight 17 · Encyclopedia Britannica, Russia-Ukraine War

2014 to 2021

The long middle war

The Minsk agreements of 2014 and 2015 froze the Donbas fighting without settling it, and the war there ground on for eight years with thousands killed. Around the same period the toolkit widened far beyond Ukraine: interference in western elections assessed by intelligence services, the NotPetya malware attack of 2017 that damaged hospitals and shipping worldwide, the nerve agent poisoning of Sergei Skripal in England in 2018, and the Wagner mercenary group fighting in Syria, Libya and the Sahel while laundering Moscow's deniability.

The sharpest warning came at home. Alexei Navalny survived poisoning with a Novichok nerve agent in August 2020, filmed the men who had tailed him including an FSB agent who confessed to the plot, flew back to Moscow in January 2021 and was arrested on landing. Navalny was then imprisoned for years on charges most observers treated as invented, and he died in an Arctic penal colony in February 2024. A state that does that to its own opposition was not going to treat a neighbor's sovereignty more gently.

Source: Encyclopedia Britannica, Russia-Ukraine War · Encyclopedia Britannica, Alexei Navalny · Encyclopedia Britannica, Wagner Group

24 February 2022

The full-scale invasion

Hours before dawn on 24 February 2022 Putin announced a special military operation, having recognized the Russian-held parts of the Donbas the night before. The stated goals were demilitarization and denazification, a government in Kyiv led by people he called drug addicts and neo Nazis, and the column of armor driving on the capital made the real plan plain: remove the government in a few days. It failed, and the war that followed is the largest in Europe since 1945.

The world's response was immediate. On 2 March 2022 the General Assembly, meeting in an emergency session, demanded Russia's complete and unconditional withdrawal from Ukraine's internationally recognized territory, with 141 states voting in favor. Russia responded with the annexation of four Ukrainian regions in September 2022 after referendums held under occupation and widely condemned, with a partial mobilization that sent hundreds of thousands of Russians out of the country, and with repeated threats to use nuclear weapons.

Source: Encyclopedia Britannica, Russia-Ukraine War · United Nations, resolution A/RES/ES-11/1 on the aggression against Ukraine

2022 onward

Atrocities documented, warrants issued

United Nations monitors were on the ground from the first weeks. They documented the killing of civilians, torture and executions, forced deportations, sexual violence and the systematic strikes on Ukraine's power and water system, and they keep publishing a monthly count that both sides are held to. The killings of civilians in Bucha, Irpin and Hostomel after Russian forces withdrew from the Kyiv region in April 2022 were documented by UN investigators and became the clearest early evidence of the war's character.

The courts followed. On 17 March 2023 the International Criminal Court issued an arrest warrant for Vladimir Putin and for Maria Lvova-Belova over the unlawful deportation and transfer of children from Ukraine to Russia, the first warrant for a sitting head of state in the court's history. Russia does not recognize the ICC and no member state has handed him over, which is why the warrant's real effect is on travel and on the record. Warrants for strikes on civilian targets have followed from other jurisdictions.

Source: United Nations, monitoring mission reports on protection of civilians in Ukraine · International Criminal Court, arrest warrants for Putin and Lvova-Belova (March 2023)

2022 onward

Inside Russia

The domestic crackdown started within days of the invasion. Legislation criminalizing what the state called fakes about the army carried up to fifteen years, discrediting the military became an administrative offense, and the independent outlets that were left closed or fled abroad. Novaya Gazeta suspended publication, TV Rain went into exile, and foreign agent and extremist labels were applied to organizations down to local human rights groups.

OVD-Info, which counts political arrests in Russia, recorded mass detentions in the first weeks of the war and has since documented criminal cases, dismissals and intimidation continuing for years. The one attempt at organized force from inside the system came from Yevgeny Prigozhin, whose Wagner columns marched on Moscow in June 2023 before turning back under a deal; he died in a plane crash two months later. Nobody was left inside Russia able to say no to the war in public.

Source: OVD-Info, three years of the anti-war movement · Encyclopedia Britannica, Alexei Navalny · Encyclopedia Britannica, Yevgeny Prigozhin

Where it stands

What is documented, and where to watch

The territorial question is not settled and this page will not pretend otherwise: several Ukrainian regions remain under Russian occupation or fighting, and the diplomatic picture changes faster than a reference page can honestly follow. What does not change is the record above: the invasion was launched without a Security Council mandate and in violation of the UN Charter, the General Assembly has demanded withdrawal repeatedly, and the evidence of war crimes is being collected by bodies that publish their findings for anyone to check.

For the present tense, use this site's War Map, which draws the front line from DeepStateMap's public snapshot with a delay of up to two to three days and a warning against using it to plan movement, plus the twice daily briefs for strikes, diplomacy and energy politics. History is settled by documents. A war is settled by what happens next.

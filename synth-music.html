Sources: The Verge, Synthtopia, MatrixSynth, Gearnews, Hispasonic, GitHub (fm1-emu, m-vave/fm1_open)

A €70 pocket synthesizer shipped with 128 presets and a locked firmware. Within months its owners had turned it into a drum machine, a groovebox, a psytrance engine, an Omnichord clone, and a piece of software that runs in any browser. Then the manufacturer open-sourced the thing it originally withheld — which is either the feel-good story of the year in music hardware, or a company watching free labour arrive and deciding to own it. The honest answer is both.

The community got there first

The M-VAVE FM-1 is a cheap FM synthesizer that went viral on forums and social media. What made it matter is what owners did next: because the platform was never officially open hardware, developers reverse-engineered it and started shipping their own firmwares anyway (Synthtopia).

The scene now includes:

  • Felucca — the original open firmware: four-operator FM synthesis with sequencer, arpeggiator and effects.
  • SLOOP and Skiff — grooveboxes, the latter with six tracks.
  • MYCELIUM — a forest/psytrance/Goa/hi-tech machine with its own drum assets, built on Felucca and SLOOP.
  • FoMni — a free, open-source chord harp in the spirit of the Suzuki Omnichord.
  • CLIPPER — an experimental build pushing the hardware into deeper polyphony.

Count them and you are near 50 community firmwares on the FM-1 Firmware Hub, with drum machines, ambient generators and more beside them. On top of that, fm1-emu — MIT-licensed — emulates the FM-1 itself, CPU, screen, keys, knobs and audio, so every one of those firmwares runs unchanged in a browser, on a computer, or as an AU/VST3 plugin in a DAW. The instrument you bought is now software you can run anywhere, forever, whether or not the company still exists.

M-VAVE's response: FM1 Open

Faced with a community that had already removed the lock, M-VAVE released FM1 Open: the application source code on GitHub under GPLv3 (with a linking exception for the SDK), a development kit, and a browser Workbench for editing projects, managing samples and flashing firmware. A second official firmware ships multiple engines — six-operator FM, a 303-style bass, an 808-style drum machine and a sampler. Versions moved from V12 to V13 within a day, and the first external contribution was already merged — per-track tuning tables in hundredths of a semitone.

The contrast the trade press keeps drawing is with Korg's microKorg2, whose firmware 2.0 opened an SDK for third-party oscillators and effects but never released the instrument's base code. M-VAVE went further — and then stopped exactly where the interesting part begins.

Read the licence, not the press release

FM1 Open's sound engine remains proprietary: no source available, licensed for use in this one device only. The effects, arrangements and demo samples remain M-VAVE's property, and the company explicitly offers no warranty over its own code — flashing is at your own risk, back up your projects first.

So the picture is: shell open, core closed. The company gets the appearance of openness, the marketing, and — if the official firmware wins the installed base — a growing pile of community-written features it can bundle and support. What it has not given up is control of the part that defines the instrument's sound. The Verge placed M-VAVE among the makers embracing the custom-firmware wave rather than suing it; Gearnews reports the same scene now reaching the Sequential Prophet-5, Arturia MicroFreak and Novation Circuit Tracks, with third-party hacks dropping weekly.

That is the model worth watching as this spreads. Instrument makers learned two lessons from the console and phone industries: openness builds a community that does your R&D for free, and you can keep the differentiator closed while harvesting it. Gearnews' public anxiety this month is about vibe-coded plugins flooding the market and losing "humanity" — worth saying plainly, the deeper threat to musicians was never AI-generated code. It is instruments you own that only the manufacturer is permitted to define.

What this means for open-source instruments

The FM-1 scene is the argument. A €70 device with a locked firmware became the most alive platform in hardware synths because its owners refused the terms they were sold — and it stayed alive because the firmwares are files you keep, the emulator is MIT-licensed, and the community's work outlives any single product decision. If M-VAVE's FM1 Open becomes the standard, it will be because it can carry a sampler and answer bug reports, not because it was the first.

The rule the whole industry is circling: if the instrument cannot run code you did not write, you are renting it.

What you can do:

  • Back the firmware authors. They are volunteers. The Hub and the developers behind Felucca, SLOOP, MYCELIUM, FoMni and CLIPPER are why your €70 box is worth €300 of capability — buy them a coffee, file a good bug report, contribute if you can code.
  • Check the licence before you flash. M-VAVE itself warns there is no warranty and tells you to back up projects and samples first; keep your stock firmware file, because that is your exit.
  • Reward openness with money. When a maker documents its bootloader, ships an SDK or releases source, that is the purchasing decision worth making — it is the only signal the industry understands.
  • Talk about it. The custom-firmware scene survives on forums, videos and repos; the moment a maker starts sending takedowns instead of SDKs, that should cost them a customer base.
